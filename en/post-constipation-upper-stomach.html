Why Constipation Can Cause Upper Stomach Symptoms

November 10, 2026 · Dr. Asher Shafrir

Patients who come to me because of discomfort in the upper stomach are often surprised when I ask about their bowel movements. Why ask about the other end? Because the digestive system is one long road, from the mouth to the rectum. When traffic stops at one end, you can feel it at the other.

Think of a traffic jam

You're driving on the highway and suddenly everything stops. There's no problem near you. But five miles ahead, there was a crash. You're stuck because of something you can't see.

Your gut works the same way. If the end of the road, the colon, is slow or full, everything behind it slows down. The stomach empties more slowly, gas and pressure build up, and you feel it in the upper belly. The parts of the gut also talk to each other through nerves, so a stretched, uncomfortable colon can make the stomach more sensitive too.

What the research shows

A study published in October 2026 in Digestive Diseases and Sciences looked at almost 300,000 Americans with gastroparesis, a condition where the stomach empties too slowly.

  • About 3 in 10 also had constipation.
  • Many people with constipation have a problem at the "exit": pelvic floor muscles that don't relax properly. Yet fewer than 2 in 100 were diagnosed with it, and very few got the test or the recommended treatment (biofeedback).
  • Those who were tested had fewer hospital stays, scans and ER visits over the next three years.

The authors also note that holding in a bowel movement slows stomach emptying. A jam at the end really can slow down the beginning. This shows that sometimes the way to treat the stomach issues is actually by treating the lower end of the road.

How I look at it in my clinic

Most people come in with one main complaint: heartburn, nausea, or a heavy feeling after meals. That's a good place to start, but it isn't the whole story. I ask about the whole digestive road. How often do you go? Is it easy? Are you bloated? Many people never mention constipation because they don't think it's related, or they've gotten used to it.

When we see all the symptoms together, the plan often changes. Sometimes treating the constipation helps the stomach too. We look for the "accident up ahead" instead of chasing each symptom on its own.

What this means for you

If you have stomach symptoms, tell your doctor about your bowel habits too, even if it feels unrelated or embarrassing.
